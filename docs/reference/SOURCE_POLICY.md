# Daily Deep Dive — Source Selection Policy

_Last updated: 17 September 2026_

Daily Deep Dive selects resources for **substance, credibility and teaching value**, not for format prestige.

A strong video can be a real source. A strong lecture, interview, documentary, podcast episode, expert explainer, book, article, paper, course or primary document can all be appropriate when they genuinely help the reader understand the topic.

## Core rule

Use a resource when it is:

- **Comprehensive enough for the role it plays in the topic**
- **Created by someone with relevant expertise, experience or unusually strong command of the subject**
- **Substantive rather than superficial**
- **Clear about evidence, uncertainty and interpretation when those distinctions matter**
- **Worth the reader's time**

Do not reject a resource simply because it is a YouTube video, podcast or other non-academic format.

Likewise, do not accept a source merely because it is a paper, book, university page or official website. Format and institutional prestige do not automatically make a source useful or correct.

## Videos and audio

Actively look for high-quality audiovisual sources when they are particularly good at teaching the subject. Suitable examples include:

- university lectures
- talks by researchers or practitioners
- expert interviews
- serious documentaries
- long-form educational videos
- conference talks
- high-quality specialist channels
- well-researched explanatory videos
- rigorous podcasts or recorded discussions

These can be used as **substantive sources**, not merely as inspiration or framing material.

A video does not need to duplicate a paper to deserve inclusion. It can add value by building intuition, connecting ideas, showing mechanisms visually, explaining a field's history, presenting an expert's synthesis, or making difficult material understandable.

## How to evaluate a video or creator

Consider:

1. **Who is speaking?** Relevant expertise, experience, track record or demonstrated command of the subject.
2. **What is the evidence base?** Does the speaker explain where claims come from or distinguish evidence from interpretation?
3. **How precise is the language?** Avoid creators who consistently turn conditional findings into universal claims.
4. **Does the content teach a mental model?** Prefer resources that help the viewer understand how the system works.
5. **Is it substantive?** Length is not the criterion; depth is.
6. **Are important limitations acknowledged?** Especially for health, science, politics, law, finance and current affairs.
7. **Is there a conflict of interest?** Sponsorship or commercial incentives do not automatically disqualify a resource, but they should be considered.

## Mixed-source topics

There is no required source hierarchy for every card. Use the combination that best teaches the topic.

For example, one deep dive might contain:

- an excellent explanatory video for the mental model
- a review paper for the evidence base
- an official source for definitions or current rules
- a book for broader context
- a critical article or competing expert perspective where genuine disagreement exists

Another topic may be best served almost entirely by books and videos. Another may require primary legal or scientific material.

The objective is **not to maximize academic-looking citations**. The objective is to give the reader the best path toward genuine understanding and independent judgment.

## User-supplied resources

When the user supplies a resource because it captures the kind of learning experience they want, treat it as a legitimate candidate source. If it is substantive and relevant, it can appear directly in the resource list and contribute to the topic's explanation.

Additional research may expand, contextualize or challenge it, but should not automatically demote it to a mere "framing resource".

The videos currently supplied for topics such as critical thinking, intelligence and ambition/consistency should therefore be treated as valid educational sources in their own right.

## Still excluded

- Wikipedia
- SEO content farms
- generic listicles
- shallow content included only to increase resource count
- repetitive resources that add no meaningful perspective
- fabricated, unverifiable or misleading links

## Guiding principle

**Choose the source a curious, intelligent person would genuinely be glad they opened.**
